My first week at my first real job, I found the gap in my education fast. I had a degree and an Excel course on my transcript, but college never offered a class on Outlook. And Outlook, it turned out, was how the entire company talked.

That memory has become my best explanation for why AI adoption is stalling inside organizations. We keep treating AI as an individual skill that each employee picks up alone, and adoption has never worked that way. AI adoption is an organization learning a new **company language**, and almost nobody is teaching it like one.

![On the left, many separate people each working with their own disconnected agent; on the right, one team connected through a single shared language network](/images/2026/08/nobody-took-an-outlook-course-banner.png)

## The Google Trap

There's a framing I hear constantly: AI is a skill you pick up like Googling. Nobody took a search class. You tried it, it worked, you kept doing it, and eventually everyone searched. AI, the argument goes, will spread the same way.

I understand the appeal, and it collapses the moment you apply it to an organization. Companies were never paying you to Google. No investment to justify, no rollout plan, no committee deciding search was strategic. Whether you used Google, Bing, or DuckDuckGo mattered to exactly nobody (your secret is safe, DuckDuckGo people). Search is a private skill with private benefits.

AI inside a company works differently. Your organization is paying real money for it, and the value only shows up when a group uses it together. I can sit right next to you and use AI brilliantly all day, and it changes NOTHING about how you do your job. There is no correlation between my AI workflow and yours unless the company builds one. A skill spreads one person at a time. A language only works when everybody speaks it.

## Nobody Taught Me Outlook

Back to that first job. I had taken an Excel course in college, and I still had to relearn Excel on the ground, because the company's data lived in Excel files and there was a way things were done with them. Outlook was the bigger surprise. How to organize messages. How to send an email properly. Who gets copied, and what a calendar invite actually commits you to. None of that was in a syllabus, because it was never a subject. It was the company language: Excel for the data, Outlook for the messaging. If that company had run on Google Workspace instead, I would have learned Gmail and Sheets. Similar tools, different dialect, same lesson. Speak the language or you cannot do the work.

Power BI became a language too, once we invested in it. There was a person talking and a person listening. Someone built the model and the report, someone consumed it, and both sides had to learn their part: where the source of truth lived, what validated meant, how a question became an answer.

Adoption is a language a group agrees to speak, backed by a common process. Access to a tool is not the same thing.

## A Pile of Private Dialects

Right now, AI in most organizations is a pile of private dialects. Everyone is fluent in their own agents, and nobody can talk to anybody else.

![On the left, a scatter of disconnected private agent nodes with no lines between them; on the right, the same nodes joined into one shared connected language network](/images/2026/08/nobody-took-an-outlook-course-dialects.png)

I'd call myself AI-ready as an individual, and plenty of practitioners would say the same. Nobody has defined what AI-ready means for an organization, for a team of people who have to produce something together. My individual skills do not transfer to the org one-to-one, and neither do my agents.

Money doesn't close the gap. You can invest heavily in AI tools and see barely any adoption, the same way publishing 200 Power BI reports never guaranteed anyone opened them. Investment is a purchase. Adoption is a behavior.

So a lot of what I'm seeing right now is **AI theater**: a solution wandering the halls looking for a problem, a copilot spun up because of a headline. The platform is rarely the blocker. Between Fabric and semantic models, the building blocks for agents are already sitting there. If an agent is going to work in your company, it runs on YOUR data, so start where the pain already is. Where do report requests still pile up? Where does someone re-key the same information every single week? Those pain points bridge naturally to something agentic, because the need is real and the data path already exists.

## The Decree Moment

When we rolled out Power BI, sales leadership told every rep, in plain language: from now on, if you want to know your quota, you go here. This is the report. Do not go digging through your old Excel files. Your targets, your goals, everything is in here. And if you have a problem with it, message Tommy.

Adoption started with that sentence, not with the licensing. The **decree** is what turned a report into the company language.

That decree did not fall out of the sky. By the time leadership said those words, someone had already decided Power BI was worth the investment, security had gotten comfortable with it, and a report existed that had been developed and validated. The decree was the last domino, not the first. Does AI have that moment in most companies today? It does not. Most organizations haven't committed to tooling, haven't cleared security, haven't built anything worth decreeing, and then they wonder why nothing gets adopted.

![A line of dominoes falling left to right, labelled tooling, then security, then a validated report, then a named owner, tipping into a final decree node](/images/2026/08/nobody-took-an-outlook-course-dominoes.png)

Before leadership can hand you a decree moment, check the dominoes:

1. Has someone with real authority decided which AI tooling the company is committing to?
2. Has security reviewed it and gotten comfortable?
3. Does a validated, working thing exist that's worth pointing people to?
4. Is there a named owner, an actual person to message when something breaks?

Every no on that list is your work. The decree comes last.

So who owns getting there? Put AI enablement inside data governance. You already have the right people in the room: IT, business intelligence, and leadership with the purse strings. It's the natural body to decide what your company's AI language will be, and to line up those four dominoes until a decree is possible.

## Treat the Agent Like a Hire

Once a team starts speaking the language, hold the agent to the standard you'd hold a new hire. What is its role? Who does it work with every day? An optional chat window off to the side is not a job.

I learned the training half of this from my own setup. I've built a stack of custom agents into my Notion workspace and written up [what each of them does](/2026/08/03/meet-my-assistants/). Drop a new teammate into that setup tomorrow and they would have no idea that changing a status triggers an agent, or that mentioning the operations agent pulls everything together. I know those things because I created them. That knowledge does not spread by osmosis. It spreads by training and shared process, exactly like Outlook etiquette did.

You'll know the language has landed when the agent shows up in the team's lingo. Did you talk to the quota agent today? Yeah, and here's what it said. When a team talks about an agent like a coworker, the agent is part of the team.

## A 30-60-90 That Starts With Data

If you asked me tomorrow for a 30-60-90 day plan to get started with AI, here's mine. It will feel slow, and it is slow for the same reason the [step-zero test](/2026/07/27/step-zero-before-the-fabric-adoption-roadmap/) feels slow: you're building the thing every roadmap assumes you already have.

![A three-phase left-to-right timeline showing data quality checks, then a discovery search across teams, then a single agent working inside one small team](/images/2026/08/nobody-took-an-outlook-course-30-60-90.png)

- **First 30 days: data quality.** Verify your data, and I mean both kinds. The hard data in your tables, and the soft data around it: definitions, process documents, the context an agent would need to be useful. Deliver findings on what's consistent and what's a mess. Scattered personal OneDrives and semantic models nobody cleaned are not the right data, and without the right data you're not going to have a great agent.
- **Days 30 to 60: discovery.** Find the team with good data and a genuine process need. Maybe it's operations, staring down the same filled-out forms week after week, sitting on data quality that's already solid. You're identifying candidates here, not building. The team you pick needs a clear reason for an agent to exist.
- **Days 60 to 90: one agent, in real use, by a real team.** An agent wired into that team's actual workflow, announced from the top, with the training to go with it. A demo doesn't count and neither does a slide that says pilot.

Three months for one agent sounds slow until you compare it to the alternative: twelve months of theater and zero adoption.

## Takeaways

- Write the one-sentence decree you wish leadership would say for a single team: if you want to know X, you go here, and if you have a problem, message me. Everything that would have to be true first is your real AI roadmap.
- Stop teaching AI as a personal skill. Pick one team and teach it as a shared language, with a common process behind it.
- Run the four-domino check before you ask leadership for that mandate: tooling chosen, security comfortable, a validated thing built, a named owner to message.
- Give AI enablement to your data governance committee. IT, BI, and the purse strings are already in that room.
- Run the plan in order: 30 days verifying hard and soft data, 30 finding the team with good data and real process pain, 30 putting one agent into that team's actual workflow with training attached.
- The agent is adopted when it turns up in how people talk. "Did you ask the quota agent?" is the finish line.

My prediction: within a few years, AI-ready stops being a line on an individual resume and becomes something we say about organizations, the way we talk about data culture today. Agent fluency will sit in onboarding right next to getting your Outlook account, and companies will teach it themselves, the way they have always taught their own language.
